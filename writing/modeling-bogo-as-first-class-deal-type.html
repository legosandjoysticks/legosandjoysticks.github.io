Product engineering note

Modeling BOGO as a first-class deal type

Grocery flyers look simple until the data gets weird. BOGO offers are a good example: they are clearly valuable to shoppers, but they do not behave like a normal shelf-price row.

In Deal Meal Planner, the tempting shortcut would be to force every promotion into a single comparable number. If an item says “buy one, get one free,” just divide the shelf price in half and rank it against everything else. That sounds clean, but it hides important product truth.

The problem with fake precision

A BOGO deal depends on quantity, household need, store rules, loyalty cards, and whether the user actually wants two of the item. Treating it as one normal unit price can make the app look more certain than the flyer really is.

For a shopper, “$2.50 each when you buy two” and “$2.50 shelf price” are not the same decision. One asks for commitment. The other does not.

The product decision

The better model is to keep BOGO as its own deal type. That lets the interface surface it clearly without pretending every promotion belongs in the same math bucket.

  • Normal price rows can rank by shelf price, savings, and recipe usefulness.
  • BOGO rows can stay visible as promotional opportunities.
  • The UI can explain the condition instead of burying it in a number.
  • The recipe planner can decide whether buying multiples actually makes sense.

The engineering shape

This pushes the system toward a cleaner API contract. Instead of one overloaded price field trying to represent every flyer row, the backend can expose fields like deal type, promo text, estimated unit price, confidence, and display label. The frontend can then make an honest product decision about how to show it.

What this taught me

The hard part of many software systems is not the screen. It is deciding what the data is allowed to mean. BOGO taught me that good product engineering often means preserving messy truth long enough to design around it, rather than sanding it down too early.
